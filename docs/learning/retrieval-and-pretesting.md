# Retrieval and pretesting

## Two different learning effects

**Strong.** Retrieval practice means attempting to recover information after learning it. A meta-analysis found that testing previously studied material generally improved later retention more than restudying it. Benefits depended on conditions, and initial recall tests produced larger benefits than recognition tests on average. This does not make every difficult response a useful retrieval attempt. [Rowland, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/).

**Moderate.** The effect extends beyond repeating exact verbal answers. Three bird-family experiments found benefits of testing for classification of studied and novel exemplars on immediate and delayed tests. Benefits depended on the balance of study and testing. This is closer to learning vegetation categories than vocabulary recall, but still differs from choosing coordinates on an unrestricted map. [Jacoby, Wahlheim, and Coane, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20804279/).

**Moderate.** Pretesting means attempting an answer before instruction. Kornell, Hays, and Bjork used fictional general-knowledge questions and weak word associates so attempts were unsuccessful. Participants then saw the correct answers. Later learning benefited relative to reading the answers without the attempt. Failure did not make the attempt useless in those tasks. The experiments did not compare months of near-zero-success landscape practice with a supported curriculum. [Kornell, Hays, and Bjork, 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/).

**Moderate.** Richland, Kornell, and Kao found that unsuccessful conceptual questions before an essay could improve subsequent learning. Pan and Carpenter's later review covers evidence from texts, lectures, videos, facts, and other materials. Benefits require a subsequent learning opportunity and vary with procedure and assessment. Pretesting can direct attention toward the queried information without improving every unqueried detail. [Richland and colleagues, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016496). [Pan and Carpenter, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5).

## What a learner needs before a guess

**Limited.** The pretesting literature does not establish that a learner needs an already correct framework before an unsuccessful attempt can help. The original experiments deliberately made many answers unavailable. Nor does it establish that any arbitrary response is equally productive. A meaningful question followed by attended corrective information is a different learning event from clicking a random place and skipping the explanation. [Kornell and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/). [Pan and Carpenter](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5).

**Inference.** A novice may lack both the visual category and the geographic alternatives needed to interpret a reveal. In that case, the reveal must supply a small model explicitly. For example, connect the visible terrain and ground to two plausible scene families. Do not infer from a low score alone that Seth has no framework, or claim that the cited studies measured this exact no-framework subgroup.

**Moderate.** Schema research explains why unguided search can occupy capacity without building useful knowledge. Sweller tested conventional problem-solving demands rather than pretesting natural images. It supplies a reason to avoid prolonged unsupported searching, not a reason to ban brief guesses before a worked explanation. [Sweller, 1988](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4).

## How to use cold attempts here

All recommendations in this section are **Inference**.

- Keep an initial unboxed pin before the reveal. Let the learner mark the attempt as no basis, tentative, or confident without pretending these are calibrated probabilities.
- Do not require an invented species name or lengthy explanation before every unfamiliar guess.
- After a no-basis miss, demonstrate one visible contrast in the reveal. If repeated no-basis attempts continue, briefly teach the broader family before another fresh test.
- Ask Seth to restate the rule without the reveal if that helps clarify it. Then classify a different unboxed scene. Remembering the sentence and seeing the cue are separate checks.
- Keep feedback clear enough to replace the wrong association. A failed attempt followed by an unread list is not the intervention tested in pretesting studies.

## Assessment versus teaching

**Inference.** A cold pretest can diagnose current confusions. A few sampled misses cannot reliably rank all US regions by weakness. A 10 to 15 minute block is defensible for a baseline if Seth accepts it, but it has no established advantage over a shorter sample. Do not spend most of every session reproducing a known 8% rate before teaching begins.

**Inference.** If the goal is clean baseline measurement, defer explanations until the short test block ends. If the goal is learning, correct after each committed answer. Do not combine an uninterrupted baseline and an explained practice block under one success percentage.

**Inference.** Use three to five cold scenes as a daily starting sample, not as a stable ranking of the three worst regions. Pool observations across sessions and keep some representative coverage. This sample size is a practical choice. Pick a small family or an unresolved contrast to study, then return to fresh pinning.

## Measurable check

**Inference.** Compare brief guess-first teaching with the same examples and explanation time but no initial guess. Assess new roads the next day. Record initial no-basis flags and later performance so the intervention can be adjusted if that group does not improve. With one learner, changing content difficulty and carryover prevent a clean population-level causal conclusion. A pretest score is a starting point, not the learning outcome.
