# Feedback: enough to change the next decision

## What the literature supports

**Strong.** Feedback helps on average, but its form matters. Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 meta-analysis covered 607 effect sizes and 23,663 observations across performance tasks. The average effect was positive, yet more than one third of effects were negative. Their feedback-intervention theory proposes that feedback becomes less useful as attention moves away from the task toward the self. The moderator findings support this account, but do not prove that every discouraging message or long explanation harms learning. Feedback sign alone did not explain the negative effects. [Kluger and DeNisi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254).

**Moderate.** Hattie and Timperley distinguish task, process, self-regulation, and self-level feedback. Their review asks where the learner is going, how the learner is doing, and what to do next. It supports explaining a useful next action rather than only issuing a grade or praising the person. This is a synthesis and framework, not an experiment comparing vegetation-card layouts. [Hattie and Timperley, 2007](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487).

**Strong.** A later educational meta-analysis found a medium average effect across 435 studies, with substantial variation. Information-rich feedback generally did better than low-information reinforcement. That finding concerns actionable information, not a prescription to show more words or more unrelated facts. It does not establish an ideal reveal length. [Wisniewski, Zierer, and Hattie, 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087).

## Elaborated versus simple feedback

**Strong.** In 40 computer-based learning studies, elaborated feedback, such as an explanation, had larger average effects than correctness-only feedback or simply supplying the answer. The advantage was particularly relevant to higher-order outcomes. These are pooled comparisons across varied tasks, not proof that a long explanation always beats a short one for the same learner. [van der Kleij, Feskens, and Eggen, 2015](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881).

**Moderate.** Shute's review recommends specific, supportive, task-focused formative feedback. It distinguishes verification, explanations, hints, and worked examples, and emphasizes interactions with learner knowledge and task demands. An expert's familiar slip may need only the correct answer. A novice who cannot see the distinction needs an explanation or demonstration. This is review-based guidance rather than a universal algorithm. [Shute, 2008](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313795).

**Inference for Fieldwork.** Use short elaboration, not bare right/wrong feedback and not an encyclopedia. A useful unit is: visible observation, geographic implication, and why it favors the answer over the actual guessed alternative. One compact relation can carry more useful information than six tree names.

## Cognitive load and cue count

**Moderate.** Sweller's problem-solving experiments and model showed how demanding solution search can compete with schema acquisition. This was problem-solving research, not a study of photo-card density. Multimedia research separately supports signaling the relevant information and keeping corresponding words and images close. Neither source establishes a maximum number of landscape features a person can learn. [Sweller, 1988](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4). [Mayer, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12197).

**Inference.** A Fieldwork miss should default to **one takeaway supported by one or two diagnostic cues in total**. Two cues can form a conjunction, such as landform plus ground moisture. This is a starting design choice, not a working-memory law or a result that two beats three in GeoGuessr. Do not count every explanatory sentence as another cue. Do not treat the number of visible panels as a measured cognitive-load score.

**Inference.** Keep the whole scene, highlight the relevant area, and put the comparison sentence beside it. Put species names, species ranges, extra trees, and full galleries behind optional detail. If no defensible cue distinguishes the places in this image, say that. A fabricated takeaway is worse than an honest ambiguity.

## When feedback should appear

**Moderate.** Feedback timing effects vary with the task, learner, and feedback content. Shute reviews cases favoring immediate correction and others favoring delay. The computer-feedback meta-analysis did not establish a reliable simple interaction that makes delayed feedback best for all higher-order work. [Shute](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313795). [van der Kleij and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881).

**Inference.** For ordinary training, collect the pin first and show corrective feedback immediately after submission. Let Seth control reading and advancing. Do not delay the explanation for an entire 15-minute failure block merely to create difficulty. Distinguish delayed feedback from a delayed review. The next fresh example should follow intervening items or a later session, not require rereading the same reveal.

**Inference.** For a clean assessment block, withhold explanations until the block ends so earlier answers do not teach later test items. Make that exception explicit. Test performance without feedback and training performance with feedback answer different questions.

## Concrete miss-card contract

All items below are **Inference**, supported by the general evidence above.

1. Name the actual region and the guessed region neutrally. Keep distance as a secondary result.
2. State one comparison rule. Point to one or two visible cues on the current full scene.
3. Show one suitably matched rival scene if it helps demonstrate the rule. Do not require inspecting every gallery.
4. Let the learner open further evidence or move on. On a later fresh scene, ask for the location and optionally the cue before any boxes appear.

**Inference check.** Compare the compact explanation with the existing reveal at equal total practice time. On different roads the next day, measure unboxed cue identification, actual pin accuracy, and false-positive cue use. Faster card dismissal alone would show less reading, not more learning.
